01

The 60-Second Answer

The instinct that this should work because the Mandarin demo is excellent is half right and half a trap: the architecture plainly can sing, but three things had to be true for the current plan to succeed and none of them are. GTSinger’s English is not 12.7 hours from 3 singers — it is 6.71 hours of actual singing from 3 singers, two of whom are altos, so in generalisation terms it is closer to two voices than to twelve hours. The fix recommended by the previous research pass — unify the phoneme frontend, as five other systems did — has no attachment point here, because VoxCPM2 is tokenizer-free and takes raw text, so there is no phone inventory to unify. And the signal used to stop training yesterday carries almost no information: the optimal diffusion loss is a nonzero, data-dependent constant, so a flat 0.4884 cannot distinguish saturation from convergence from undertraining, and the closest published analogue found validation loss and perceptual quality moving in opposite directions. Meanwhile nobody — not one person — has ever published a cross-lingual VoxCPM finetune or an English checkpoint for any Chinese-origin singing model, so there is no recipe to copy and no evidence the low-data path is quietly working elsewhere. The one genuinely encouraging precedent, BiSinger, got bilingual Chinese/English singing from just 1.91 hours of real English singing — by manufacturing 38 hours more with voice conversion and pitch-shifting English speech into pseudo-singing. And a model that already does the whole job exists: SoulX-Singer is Apache-2.0, natively English, takes MIDI scores, and clones timbre zero-shot — though its quality claims are entirely self-reported.

Reliability: Medium-high
8/10

Three independent mechanisms say the number carried almost no information. The optimal diffusion loss is nonzero and dataset-dependent, so a flat value cannot distinguish “hit capacity” from “converged to the irreducible optimum” from “still undertrained” — and the loss-gap-to-quality correlation changes sign across noise levels. Timestep gradient variance spans up to 100×, so a ~32-sample batch mean stabilises to four decimals long before learning stops. And the closest published analogue — LoRA on an LLM-based TTS backbone at 3.5–18.5 h per speaker, bracketing our 12.7 h — found validation loss improving while DNS-MOS degraded, concluding that checkpoint selection “must be guided by perceptual evaluation rather than loss convergence alone.” The rising stop-head loss cited as the second reason is a known pathology of a one-positive-frame-per-utterance classifier. Capped at 8 because this shows the decision was unfounded, not that the model was still improving. Improved DDPM genuinely documents diffusion degrading with long training — detected by FID, not loss.

Reliability: High
9/10

Whisper’s own error rate on sung audio is 35–45 % WER (Jam-ALT: large-v2 35.7 %, large-v3 35.5 %, English v3 37.7 %) — the same magnitude as our entire measured signal of 41–46 %. Vocal separation generally made it worse. Two careful human reference versions of the same lyrics differ by 11.1 % WER; our references are unverified Whisper output, so they are worse than that. At WER ≈ 0.45 over 141 words the binomial standard error alone is ±4.2 pp per run, before within-utterance clustering — which precisely predicts the 29.79/56.74 swing observed on one model. Real papers use 802–2,000 items; F5-TTS averages three seeds; VocalRender itself pins no seed and states no item count.

“15–30 minutes is enough for cross-lingual singing transfer”

A BA thesis reports English→German DiffSinger transfer at 15 and 30 minutes performing “comparable or even better” than large-scale monolingual training. It is the smallest successful cross-lingual singing adaptation on record and it is tempting — but the PDF would not parse, the numbers are unverified, and the task class is different: one target singer, an explicit phoneme mapping, a small acoustic model, reconstruction metrics. VocalRender’s goal is open-domain prompt-conditioned generation of arbitrary English lyrics from a 2.3B codec LM. Do not plan around the 15-minute number.

03

The Hidden Connection

Four results looked like separate disappointments: a 151M-parameter bug whose fix changed nothing measurable, a loss that went flat almost immediately, an evaluation that cannot separate any condition, and a rank increase that bought nothing.

They are one result seen four ways. Every one of them is what it looks like when the data, not the training, is the binding constraint. Fixing a bug in a system that has already extracted everything 6.71 hours from two voice types contains produces exactly zero measurable change — which is precisely what happened. The loss flattens because the model reached the irreducible optimum for that data. The evaluation cannot separate conditions because the conditions genuinely differ by less than its resolution. And the rank increase does nothing because capacity was never the limit.

Every failure this project has hit is the same failure wearing a different mask — which is oddly good news, because it means there is one problem to solve rather than four, and BiSinger already showed it can be solved by manufacturing data rather than finding it.

04

The Five Questions, Answered

Q1 — What do the authors say?

Almost nothing. One sentence, in HuggingFace discussion #1: “We just train the model on Chinese because of data and computing limitation. You may train your own checkpoint with target language.” No roadmap, no recipe, no planned English checkpoint. The repo has one issue, zero replies, zero PRs, and 16 commits none of which touch multilingual work. The paper has no v2 and does not list language as a limitation. No Chinese-language write-up exists anywhere.

The useful guidance comes from the base model’s maintainers instead, and three items are directly relevant: upstream VoxCPM2’s default LoRA config is r=32, α=32, LM+DiT, LR 1e-4, 1,000 iterations — so our “plateau by step 1000” matches the vendor’s intended schedule rather than signalling failure; a maintainer describes stop-loss converging far faster than diffusion loss as a known failure mode with known fixes, which is exactly our symptom; and another prescribes mixing original-language data and building code-switched utterances to prevent forgetting.

Q2 — What has the community achieved?

For this model, nothing at all. For the neighbours, the pattern is consistent: every successful cross-lingual recipe makes the phone or vocab set an explicit first step, and every documented failure is phonetic or catastrophic rather than merely low-fidelity — 15 h Turkish gave “just unintelligible noise”, ~60 h gave output that “did not resemble English at all”, a Czech from-scratch attempt died on “wrong reading of J”. Four-for-four in each direction, correlational, and none of it transfers cleanly to a tokenizer-free base.

Q3 — How do you build an evaluation that works?

Concretely, from the papers: ≥300 distinct segments and ≥2,500 reference words per condition (real papers use 802–2,000 items); deduplicate — five identical lyric lines means our effective n was ~11, not 15; pin the seed and average ≥3 seeds, the F5-TTS protocol; report CER as headline with WER alongside, since CER is materially more stable on short utterances; test significance with a blockwise bootstrap on the difference, blocked by song, 1,000 replicates, 95 % CI, using the existing open implementation; replace ASR references with verified lyrics, or adopt SoulX-Singer’s reconciliation against the written lyric; and transcribe with two ASR families, since evaluator choice alone can reverse rankings. Under that rule the current base/r16/r32 result is correctly reported as “no measurable difference” — which was the honest finding.

Q5 — Is this achievable at all?

Not as originally framed. Continuing to finetune 2.3B parameters on 6.71 h from two timbre classes is the one option the evidence rules out. Every published multilingual singing success — TCSinger 2, Transinger, SoulX-Singer — got there by multilingual pretraining from the start, not by adding a language afterward. Accent is recoverable from multilingual model internals and is mitigated by accent diversity, which three singers cannot supply, so a persistent Mandarin-inflected English is the expected outcome even if everything else goes right.

But two narrower framings are live, and both are supported. First, use a model that already sings English — SoulX-Singer takes the exact data format and needs no training. Second, if training is the point, do what BiSinger did: manufacture the data rather than collect it, and mix original-language data back in. The honest reading is that the goal is reachable and the method was wrong — not that the goal is impossible.

05

What To Actually Do

Ordered so the cheapest things that could change the plan come first. Steps 1–3 cost hours, not days.

01
Try SoulX-Singer before writing another line of training code.

Apache-2.0, English-native, takes MIDI scores, zero-shot timbre. Feed it one of the existing annotated songs and a reference clip. If it sounds good, the entire finetuning programme is unnecessary and the remaining work is a pipeline, not a research project. If it sounds bad, that is worth knowing in an afternoon rather than a month. Its claims are self-reported, so this is a test, not an endorsement.

02
Listen to the checkpoints already on disk.

The step-1000, 1500 and 2000 adapters were judged on a signal that carries almost no information. Render the same phrases from all three plus base, and listen. This directly tests whether “saturates at 1000 steps” was ever true, and may reverse the stop decision.

03
Probe for English forgetting — a 20-minute experiment.

Run the Mandarin-finetuned VocalRender as plain English TTS and compare against stock VoxCPM2. If English degraded, the Mandarin singing finetune damaged it, and the fix is replay — mixing Mandarin data back in, exactly as a VoxCPM maintainer prescribes — not more English. This discriminates sharply between two very different root causes, and no paper can answer it for you.

04
Rebuild the evaluation before running any more experiments.

The 180-segment GTSinger English val set is already on disk and unused. Deduplicate, target ≥2,500 reference words, pin seeds and average three, report CER alongside WER with a blockwise-bootstrap CI, and use verified lyrics rather than Whisper output as ground truth. Without this, no future result is interpretable — and Whisper’s 35–45 % floor on sung audio means it should not be the sole judge.

05
If training continues: manufacture data, don’t collect it.

BiSinger turned 1.91 h of real English singing into 38.2 h via singing-voice conversion across 20 target singers, and separately pitch-shifted 5.68 h of English speech into 17.5 h of pseudo-singing. Both routes are open here — English speech corpora are abundant and RVC-class conversion is mature. This also directly attacks the two-voice problem, which more hours from the same three singers cannot.

06
Mix Mandarin back in, and reweight the stop head.

Both come from VoxCPM maintainers responding to this exact class of problem: mix 10–20 % original-language data to prevent forgetting, and treat a stop head that converges far faster than the diffusion loss as a known defect to be reweighted rather than a reason to stop. Cheap config changes on the next run.

07
Keep NNSVS/DiffSinger as the fallback, not the plan.

Its English support documents 1 h prototype / 4 h decent / 8 h+ very high quality, and 6.71 h clears the middle tier. DiffSinger trained in ~28 GPU-hours on a single V100. It takes lyrics+MIDI and gives per-voice control. The cost is one training cycle per voice and no zero-shot timbre — a different project, but a documented-achievable one.

08

The Frontier Question

Is SoulX-Singer’s English actually good — and if so, why is anyone still training?

Every quality claim about it is first-party, measured on its own benchmark, with no independent replication and about a thousand downloads a month for a model claiming 42,000 hours of training data and state-of-the-art multilingual singing. Either the field has not noticed it yet, or it does not live up to the paper. Both are plausible and the difference is enormous: one outcome makes the whole finetuning programme unnecessary, the other confirms that open-weight English singing with zero-shot timbre remains unsolved and the hard path is the only path.

No lens could settle it because the evidence does not exist — and unlike most frontier questions, this one is answerable in an afternoon with a reference clip and one annotated song already sitting on disk. It should be the next thing done, before any further training, because it is the only cheap experiment whose result changes everything downstream.
